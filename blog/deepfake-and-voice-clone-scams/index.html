Deepfake and voice-clone scams aimed at small businesses: how to protect yours

By AI First Move · November 18, 2026 · All articles

Scammers can now clone your voice from a short audio clip and use it to call your bookkeeper, pretending to be you. They can fake a video of your face approving a wire transfer. Small businesses are attractive targets because they move fast, trust each other and often lack formal approval steps. Here's how to close those gaps before someone uses them.

Key takeaways

  • A deepfake is a fake photo, video or audio clip made with AI that shows a real person saying or doing something they never did. A voice clone is a copy of someone's voice built from a short recording, often taken from public videos or voicemails.
  • Small businesses are targeted partly because many don't have security departments, but they do have bank accounts and payroll.
  • A "safe word" between you and your team costs nothing and can stop many impersonation attempts.
  • If you suspect an attack, the first hour matters. Know who to call before it happens.

Why your small business is a more attractive target than you think

Big corporations have IT teams, fraud detection software and several layers of approval. You probably don't. That makes you easier to hit.

Your employees trust you. If whoever handles payments gets a voicemail that sounds exactly like you asking for an urgent wire, they may well do it. The urgency is manufactured on purpose; scammers know that pressure gets in the way of judgment. They count on your team not stopping to question the boss's voice.

Your public presence also works against you. Interviews on local news, YouTube walkthroughs, LinkedIn videos, podcast appearances: all of it is free raw material for a voice-cloning tool. Scammers don't need much; they need enough to fake a short call.

The scale is real. The FBI's latest annual internet crime report has a section on artificial intelligence for the first time, covering 22,364 complaints and nearly $893 million in losses, and the FBI lists voice clones and believable fake videos among the tools scammers use.[1]

Step 1: Check what scammers can already find about you

Search your own name and your senior team members' names on YouTube, LinkedIn and Google. You're looking for audio and video where voices are clear and faces are visible. That's the raw material for a clone.

You don't need to remove yourself from the internet. That's not realistic. But you can make deliberate choices:

  • Set older podcast or interview clips to unlisted or private if they no longer bring in business.
  • Take long videos of one person speaking out of public view where you can, since those are the most useful for copying a voice.
  • Check your voicemail greeting. A scammer can call your business line just to collect recordings of your voice.
  • Review what details are visible on LinkedIn: your direct phone number, how your email addresses are formed, who reports to whom. Scammers build a picture before they call.

This check doesn't take long. Do it once, then repeat it every six months.

Step 2: Set up a spoken check before you need it

This is one of the most practical things you can do, and it's free. Pick a "safe word" or short phrase that only you and your key team members know, and change it from time to time. Anyone who calls claiming to be you, or claiming to be a supplier or bank contact in an emergency, has to give that word before any money moves. The FBI gives families the same advice: agree on a secret word or phrase to confirm who is really calling.[2]

Keep it simple. A single word or a short phrase works fine. Then write a short policy (one page is enough) saying that any wire transfer, payroll change or new payment instruction requires:

  • A second confirmation through a different channel. If the request came by phone, hang up and call the person back on a number you already have. The Federal Trade Commission's advice on cloned-voice calls is the same: call the person on a number you know is theirs.[3]
  • The safe word, spoken or typed.
  • A second approver for any payment over an amount your business sets.

Yes, this slows things down slightly. That's the point.

Step 3: Teach your team what manipulation sounds like

Your team's instincts are your first line of defense. Scammers rely on pressure, not just fake voices. Train your team to recognize these patterns:

  • Manufactured urgency: "I need this done in the next 15 minutes or we lose the deal." Real emergencies rarely require skipping your normal process entirely.
  • Requests for secrecy: "Don't tell anyone about this yet, it's confidential." A genuine instruction from a real boss survives a second pair of eyes.
  • Emotional pressure: Scammers sometimes pose as a distressed owner or family member. The emotion is there to push careful thinking aside.
  • Switching channels: A scam often starts in one place (email) and moves to another (phone) to look more genuine.
  • Requests that break your normal pattern: If you've never wired money to a new supplier without a purchase order, that shouldn't start today because someone called.

Run a short drill once or twice a year. Send a made-up "urgent" request to your team from an unfamiliar number and see who follows the steps. No blame, no punishment. Just a chance to learn.

Step 4: Tighten how money requests move inside your business

How payment requests travel inside your business matters as much as how they arrive from outside. A few changes make impersonation harder:

  • Agree on one channel your team uses for payment requests, so a request that arrives any other way stands out.
  • Turn on two-step sign-in for your business email and banking. The US cybersecurity agency CISA advises against relying on text-message codes: a free authenticator app is better, and a physical security key or passkey is the option that resists phishing.[4]
  • Separate the person who requests a payment from the person who approves it. Even in a three-person business, this split matters.
  • Write down your bank's real fraud phone numbers and keep the list somewhere offline, so when you need to call fast you're not searching online and landing on a fake number.

Step 5: Know your first hour if something goes wrong

Prevention sometimes fails. What you do in the first hour after a suspected deepfake or voice-clone attack can decide whether money is recovered. The times below are a guide, not a rule. Write the steps down and post them somewhere in your office.

  • First 10 minutes: Don't confirm or deny anything to the caller. End the call. Phone your bank using the number on your card or statement, not a number given during the suspicious call. If money has moved, ask for a recall; if not, ask them to watch the account.
  • Next 10 minutes: Talk to the team member who was contacted. Collect every detail: what number called, what was said, what was done. Write it down while it's fresh.
  • Next 10 minutes: File a complaint with the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center at ic3.gov. This creates a record, and the FBI can work with banks to try to freeze stolen funds.[5]
  • Rest of the hour: Contact your business insurer if you carry a cyber or crime policy. Tell your accountant if funds moved. Change the passwords for any accounts that were mentioned in the call.

Speed matters for getting money back. For a wire transfer, the order is: your bank first, to ask for a recall, then IC3 straight away.[5]

What does a low-cost setup actually look like?

You don't need expensive software. A realistic small-business setup might include:

Layer What to use Cost
Payment checks Written policy: call back on a known number, safe word, second approver Free
Two-step sign-in Authenticator app on your phone Free[4]
Stronger sign-in Physical security key or passkey A passkey is free; a security key is a one-time purchase
Reporting FBI IC3 (ic3.gov) and the FTC (reportfraud.ftc.gov) Free
Insurance Cyber or crime cover added to your business policy Varies; ask your insurer whether impersonation fraud is covered

None of this needs a technical background to set up.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if a call is using a cloned voice?

You often can't tell while it's happening. Voice cloning has improved a great deal, and short calls are especially hard to judge by ear. That's why your protection has to be a process, not your hearing. Don't try to spot a fake voice; build steps that make the fake voice useless.

Do I need to worry about video deepfakes, or just voice?

Both. Voice cloning is common in business fraud because a phone call is an easy way in. But fake video is used too, including on live video calls. In 2024 the engineering firm Arup confirmed that an employee in Hong Kong paid out $25 million after a video call with a deepfake of a senior executive.[6] The FBI suggests looking for small flaws such as odd-looking faces, lag and unnatural movement,[2] but don't rely on spotting them. If anything feels off, confirm through a different channel before you act.

Can my bank reverse a wire transfer if I was scammed?

Sometimes, but only if you act fast. Contact your bank immediately and ask for a recall. The sooner you act, the better your chances. This is why having your bank's fraud number written down in advance matters so much.

Should I report a deepfake scam even if no money was lost?

Yes. Report it to the FBI's IC3 (ic3.gov) and to the FTC (reportfraud.ftc.gov).[3] Reports of attempts help investigators see patterns, and a report on file can support an insurance claim if the scammers try again.

How do scammers get enough audio to clone my voice?

They need very little. The security company McAfee reported that its researchers needed only about three seconds of audio to produce a clone.[7] Voicemail greetings, YouTube videos, podcast appearances and live streams are all available to anyone, with no access to your systems. That's why trimming what's public is a practical first step.

Build the habit, not just the checklist

A written policy that nobody reads won't protect you. What protects you is a team that has talked about this, practiced it and knows what to do when something feels wrong. That starts with you saying out loud: "If anyone ever calls claiming to be me and asks you to move money, here's exactly what to do."

You don't need to become a security expert. You need a few habits that make impersonation hard enough that scammers move on to easier targets.
