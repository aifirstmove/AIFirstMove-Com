How to build an AI assistant that already knows your business

By AI First Move · November 17, 2026 · All articles

Tools and features checked November 17, 2026

You can build an AI assistant that knows your products, your tone, and your policies by writing a plain-text set of instructions, attaching a few reference documents, and saving both inside an AI tool that keeps them together. No code required. Plan on a focused block of time for the first build; upkeep takes far less.

Key takeaways

  • Well-written instructions do most of the work. The tool matters less than what you put into it.
  • Your reference documents are what separate a generic AI from one that knows your business.
  • Test every assistant on real tasks before you let it touch anything that goes to a customer.
  • The names are changing: most ChatGPT accounts lose their custom GPTs on December 11, 2026,[1][2] so in ChatGPT use a Project instead.

Two terms worth pinning down before you start

A custom assistant is an AI assistant set up ahead of time with your own instructions and documents, so it behaves consistently for a defined purpose: answering customer questions in your brand voice, for example, instead of a generic one. Context is everything the assistant can "see" at once: your instructions, your uploaded files, and the current conversation. When people say an AI "forgot" something, they usually mean the item fell outside that context, or was never added in the first place.

You don't need a developer. You do need a few things ready before you open any AI tool.

Step 1: Write instructions that tell the AI who it is

Your instructions are the single most important thing you'll create. A vague instruction like "help our customers" produces a vague assistant. Specific instructions produce specific, useful responses.

Open a plain text document and write in direct sentences. Cover these four areas. The examples use a made-up flooring store:

  • Role: What is this assistant for? ("You handle first-response questions from customers of our flooring store. You don't discuss pricing; send pricing questions to the sales team.")
  • Tone: How should it sound? ("Write like a knowledgeable but relaxed store associate: no corporate jargon, no exclamation marks.")
  • Boundaries: What should it never do? ("Never quote lead times. Never promise a specific delivery date.")
  • Fallback: What happens when it doesn't know? ("If you're unsure, say: 'I don't have that detail. Let me connect you with the team.' Then stop.")

Keep the instructions under one page. Longer isn't better; clearer is better. And read them out loud: if a sentence sounds like a legal notice, rewrite it in plain language so the AI doesn't start sounding like one too.

Step 2: Choose and prepare your reference documents

This is where the assistant stops being generic. Your reference documents give it real facts to draw from instead of plausible-sounding guesses.

Start with your two most-used documents. For many small businesses, those are:

  • A product or service list with plain descriptions (plain facts, not marketing copy)
  • Your FAQ page or a typed list of the ten questions you answer every week

Format matters. Plain text or simple PDF files work best; heavily formatted spreadsheets or image-heavy PDFs are harder for most tools to read cleanly. If your document has a lot of tables or columns, copy the content into a plain text file first.

Keep each document current. An assistant working from an outdated price list will give wrong answers with total confidence. Set a calendar reminder to swap in updated files; quarterly is a reasonable starting point.

Step 3: Set it up in your chosen tool

The menus differ by tool and change with updates, so this section stays general on purpose. The method is the same in a ChatGPT Project, a Claude Project, a Microsoft Copilot agent or a Gemini skill:

  1. Create a new project, agent or skill inside your tool.
  2. Paste your instructions into the instructions field (sometimes labeled custom instructions).
  3. Upload your reference documents to the same place.
  4. Give it a specific name ("Customer questions, flooring store") so you don't confuse it with others later.

That's the setup. The assistant now starts every conversation there already knowing your instructions and with your documents on hand; you don't re-enter any of it each time.

Step 4: Test it on real questions before anyone else uses it

Testing isn't optional. Use the five test prompts from your list. For each one, ask yourself three things:

  • Did it get the facts right?
  • Did it sound like your business, not a generic chatbot?
  • Did it stay within its boundaries (no pricing quotes if you told it not to give them, no invented delivery times)?

If it fails on any of those, fix the instructions. A wrong or off-brand answer usually means there is a gap in what you wrote, not that the tool is broken. Add a sentence that covers the gap and test again.

One rule worth keeping: read every draft the assistant produces before it reaches a customer. Every time, at first. After a few weeks of consistent results on a specific task you can start spot-checking instead, but don't skip this phase early on.

What goes wrong, and how to fix it

Here are four common problems.

The assistant ignores your instructions and answers like a generic AI. Your instructions are probably too vague. Go back and add specifics: exact phrases to use, exact topics to avoid, and an explicit fallback sentence for anything outside its scope.

It gives confident but wrong answers about your products. Your reference documents either aren't uploaded, aren't being read cleanly, or are out of date. Check that your files are attached to the project itself, not just sitting on your device, and that they're in a format the tool can read (plain text or clean PDF).

The tone is off: it sounds stiff, overly formal, or too casual. Tone instructions need examples, not just adjectives. Don't write "sound friendly." Write: "Match this sentence: 'Happy to help. Here's what I'd suggest.'" One example beats ten adjectives.

It works in testing but drifts in long conversations. This is a context issue. In very long conversations, the AI can lose track of its early instructions; our article on why AI forgets what you told it explains why. Keep your instructions concise, and start a fresh chat for a new task.

Keeping it useful over time

A custom assistant isn't a one-time project. Plan on two kinds of upkeep:

  • When something goes wrong: any time you catch a wrong answer, update the relevant document or instruction that same day. Don't let errors pile up.
  • On a schedule: review it every quarter. Check that your product list, prices, and policy documents are still current, and swap in updated versions when they're not.

Skipping upkeep is an easy mistake to make. An assistant working from stale information doesn't fail quietly; it gives wrong answers with the same confidence it always did.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a paid subscription to any of these tools?

Not to get started. Claude's free plan allows up to five projects.[4] A free ChatGPT account can use Projects, with five files per project.[3] With Microsoft, organizations already on Microsoft 365 can use agents that rely on instructions or public websites at no extra cost; reading your company's own shared content is where charges begin, through a Microsoft 365 Copilot license or usage-based billing.[7] These limits were current on November 17, 2026, and can change.

How much of my business information is safe to upload?

Don't upload anything you wouldn't hand to an outside contractor: no customer personal data, no bank or payment details, no employee Social Security numbers. Plain business documents such as product lists, FAQs and brand guidelines are generally fine. On personal plans, free or paid, what you put in can be used to train the company's models unless you switch that setting off; business plans do not train on your data by default.[10] Read the tool's data settings before uploading anything sensitive.

Can one assistant handle every task?

Not well. An assistant trying to do customer support, internal HR questions, and marketing copy at once tends to do all three poorly. Build separate assistants for separate tasks; each one gets focused instructions and the right documents for its job.

What if the AI tool I'm using changes its features?

Your instructions and your reference documents are yours, so keep copies outside the tool. If a platform changes how its projects or agents work, as happened with Gems on November 17, 2026, you can rebuild quickly by pasting your saved instructions into the new setup. The method travels; the menus don't.

Start with the task you already dread

You don't need a perfect setup on day one. Pick the one task that eats your time every single week: the customer question you answer twelve times before lunch, the internal FAQ nobody can ever find, the product description someone has to explain from scratch every time. Build an assistant for that one thing. Get it right. Then build the next one.

Small, well-scoped assistants you trust beat ambitious ones you stop using.
