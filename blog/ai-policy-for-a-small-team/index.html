Your staff are already using AI: a one-page policy for a small team

By AI First Move · November 24, 2026 · All articles

Your team is probably already pasting customer emails into ChatGPT, using AI to write proposals, and running AI features inside apps you're paying for. That's likely happening right now, with or without your sign-off. A one-page AI policy won't stop any of that. What it does is give everyone a shared set of rules so you're not discovering a data problem after it's too late.

Key takeaways

  • Shadow AI is what happens when staff use AI tools privately, without your knowledge. A written policy brings that activity into the open where you can manage it.
  • A big risk for small teams isn't an AI making a bad decision. It's an employee pasting personally identifiable information (PII) into a consumer AI tool that may train on what you enter.
  • A human-in-the-loop requirement means no AI output goes directly to a customer or into a business record without a person reviewing it first.
  • Hallucination is the term for an AI stating something confidently that is simply not true. Your policy needs to name this risk so staff know to check facts.
  • A policy that fits on one page is more likely to get read. A long document tends to get filed and forgotten.
  • AI features built into software you already pay for are a common blind spot. Many subscriptions now have AI features tucked away in their settings.
  • Your policy needs a review date built in. AI tools and vendor terms change fast, and a policy written today may be outdated within months.

Terms you'll see in this article

These definitions are written for a non-technical reader. Each one matters to your policy.

Shadow AI is AI tool use that happens inside a business without the owner's knowledge or approval. An employee using the free version of an AI assistant in a personal browser tab is a classic example. It's not rogue behavior. It's unmanaged behavior.

PII (personally identifiable information) is any information that can identify a specific person. Names, email addresses, phone numbers, home addresses and account numbers all count, and health details about a person are a stricter kind. Pasting PII into an unapproved AI tool is a common risk for small teams.

Data retention is how long an AI provider keeps the content you submit. A separate question is whether your chats are used to train future models. Both depend on the product, the plan and your settings, so you need to check.

Hallucination is when an AI tool produces content that sounds authoritative but is factually wrong. It might cite a law that doesn't exist, invent a competitor's product specification, or write a figure into a proposal that nobody can verify. Staff need to know this happens so they check outputs before using them. Our post on why AI gets things wrong gives a simple way to check.

Human in the loop means a real person reviews AI output before it takes effect. Customer-facing content, financial documents, HR correspondence, and anything that could affect someone's rights or expectations all need a human check.

Why you can't wait until you're "ready"

There's no ready. AI tools are likely already part of your team's daily work, and waiting for a perfect moment to write a policy means you're managing the risk blind the whole time.

Shadow AI isn't a new problem, but it has become much easier to fall into. AI features now appear inside tools your team already uses: project management platforms, email, customer support software, design apps. Some of those features are on by default. That means your staff may be using AI whether or not anyone has thought about it, and in some cases the vendor's settings decide what happens to your business data.

The goal of an AI policy at this stage isn't to slow anyone down. It's to draw a clear line between what's fine and what's a real risk. Your bookkeeper pasting a client's tax ID into a consumer AI chat window is a risk. Your marketing coordinator using AI to draft social posts from a brief you've approved isn't. The difference matters, and your team can't tell the difference if you haven't told them.

Small teams also tend to trust each other, which is a strength until it isn't. When everyone assumes someone else has thought through the tool they're using, nobody has. A one-page policy writes down what everyone had been assuming. That's the entire point.

The one-page policy you can copy right now

Copy the text below, add your business name and date, and adjust anything that doesn't fit your situation. This is a starting point, not legal advice.

[Business name] AI use policy
Effective date: [date]
Review date: [six months from the effective date]

Why this policy exists
AI tools help us work faster, but they carry real risks if used without care. This policy gives every team member a clear set of rules to follow so we protect our customers, our business and each other.

What counts as an AI tool
Any feature or product that uses AI to generate text, images, code or decisions. This includes standalone apps such as AI chat assistants as well as AI features built into tools we already use.

Before you use any AI tool at work

  • Check that it's on the approved list below. If it isn't, ask before you use it.
  • Use a business account, not a personal account, wherever that option exists.
  • Check the tool's privacy settings and confirm it doesn't train on what you enter.

What you must never put into any AI tool

  • Passwords, sign-in details or software access keys
  • Payment card numbers, bank details or tax ID numbers
  • Health information about any person
  • Confidential contracts or unreleased business plans
  • Employee personal details, including pay or health information

Customer details
Customer names, email addresses, phone numbers and other personal details go only into tools on the approved list. Even there, take the identifying details out first whenever the job allows.

Human review is required before

  • Any AI-generated content goes to a customer
  • Any AI-generated figure appears in a document we share outside the business
  • Any AI recommendation affects a business or hiring decision

Remember: AI can be confidently wrong. Always fact-check anything you wouldn't have written yourself.

Approved tools
[List the tools here, with the account type for each, for example "ChatGPT Business account, signed in with your work email"]

How to get a new tool approved
Email [name or role] with the tool's name, what you'd use it for, and a link to its privacy policy. You'll get a yes or no within [number] business days.

What to do if something goes wrong
If you think you've accidentally shared sensitive data, made an error using AI, or noticed an AI tool behaving unexpectedly, tell [name or role] the same day. No blame. We just need to know fast.

Breaking this policy
Intentional or repeated violations will be handled through our standard HR process.

Questions? Talk to [name or role].

How to introduce the policy without pushback

How you present this policy decides whether staff treat it as a useful tool or as surveillance. The difference comes down to framing.

Don't send it as an attachment with no context. That's the fastest way to get people to sign it without reading it. Instead, set aside time in your next team meeting to walk through it out loud. Read the "never put into any AI tool" list together. Ask whether anyone is using a tool that isn't on the approved list. You'll likely learn things you didn't know, and staff will feel included rather than policed.

Name the problem you're solving, not the punishment you're threatening. Something like: "We want you to keep using AI, because it saves time. But it's easy for someone to share customer data without meaning to, and that's the problem we're preventing here." That's specific, non-accusatory and clear.

Keep the approval process fast. If a team member has to wait weeks for a tool to be approved, they'll be tempted to use it without asking next time. A quick answer to approval requests is a realistic target for a small team, and it shows the policy isn't there to slow people down.

And revisit it out loud, not just on paper. When you review the policy at the six-month mark, say so in a team meeting. That shows the policy is a living document, not a legal exercise you've filed and forgotten.

How to vet AI tools before you say yes

Vetting a tool doesn't require a legal team. It requires asking four questions and knowing where to find the answers.

Does it train on what you enter by default? Look for a section in the tool's privacy policy on data use or model training. If your inputs are used to improve the model, that's a flag. Many providers offer business accounts where this is off by default, which is worth knowing before you approve a consumer plan instead. Our post Is it safe to put customer or business information into an AI tool? sets out how the main assistants handle this, plan by plan.

Who owns the outputs? Some AI tools claim a license to use or display content you generate. For most business uses that's fine. For work involving confidential processes or creative assets, check the terms.

Where is data stored and for how long? Data retention policies vary widely. Some providers delete inputs quickly, and others keep them for weeks or longer. Find the specific retention period before approving any tool that employees might use with real business content.

What happens when the tool makes a mistake? Read the liability clauses. Many AI providers disclaim responsibility for errors in outputs. That's common, but it means your team needs to know that reviewing outputs isn't optional. The provider is unlikely to cover your losses if an AI-generated figure ends up in a client invoice.

When a software service you already pay for adds an AI feature, don't assume your existing terms cover it. Vendors often update their data-handling terms when they add AI. It's worth a quick check each time you get a notice about new AI features in a product you use.

What to do when something goes wrong

Something will probably go wrong at some point. Plan for it now so your team isn't improvising late on a Friday.

Two common incidents for small teams are sensitive data going into the wrong tool and an AI hallucination making its way into a document that leaves the business. Both are manageable if you catch them fast.

If sensitive data was submitted to an unapproved tool:

  • Stop using the tool immediately.
  • Write down exactly what was submitted.
  • Check the tool's data deletion options. Many providers offer a way to delete chats or request deletion of submitted content.
  • Tell the business owner or the named contact the same day.
  • If customer PII was involved, consider whether the affected customers need to be told. This depends on your situation and is worth a conversation with a legal or privacy professional if you're unsure.

If an AI-generated error went out to a customer:

  • Work out exactly what was sent and to whom.
  • Correct the error directly with the customer.
  • Write down what happened and add a step to your review process so it doesn't happen again.

A no-blame approach matters here. If staff fear punishment for reporting mistakes, they're less likely to report them. Fast reporting gives you options, and delayed reporting takes them away.

Keeping the policy current

A policy written in January may be out of date by July. Set a calendar reminder six months from your effective date and set aside time to review these things:

  • Are all approved tools still on suitable business plans with the right data settings?
  • Has any vendor updated its privacy policy or data retention terms?
  • Are there new tools your team is using that need a formal yes or no?
  • Has an AI feature been added to your existing software without you noticing?
  • Have there been any incidents, near-misses or staff questions that point to a gap in the current rules?

Update the document, update the date, and walk the team through any changes. That short review is what separates a living policy from a document that creates false confidence.

Frequently asked questions

Do I really need a written policy if my team is small?

Yes. Small teams often have less formal oversight, which makes unmanaged AI use more likely, not less. A one-page written policy gives everyone the same reference point. Without it, every person on your team is making their own call every time they use a tool.

Can I just tell staff not to use AI instead of writing a policy?

You can try, but it's unlikely to work. AI features are now built into tools your team already uses, often switched on by default. A blanket ban tends to push the behavior out of sight and make it harder to manage.

What's the fastest way to roll out a policy to a team of five people?

Read through it together in a short meeting. Answer questions. Put it somewhere everyone can find it, like a shared drive or a pinned message in your team chat. Ask everyone to confirm they've read it. That's the rollout.

Does using a business plan really make a difference to data privacy?

Often yes, but check the specific terms. Business and enterprise plans from the main AI providers do not use what you enter to train their models by default. Personal plans, free or paid, often do unless you switch it off. The plan type isn't a guarantee. It's a starting point that you confirm by reading the data terms for that specific product.

How do I handle an employee who keeps using unapproved tools?

Address it through your standard performance process, the same way you'd handle any other policy breach. The key is making sure the policy was clearly communicated and that the employee confirmed they understood it. That's why the rollout meeting and the written confirmation matter.

How often should I update my AI policy?

Every six months is a reasonable minimum. If a tool you've approved changes its terms, releases a feature that changes how data is handled, or is bought by another company, review straight away and don't wait for the scheduled date.

Start with the conversation

You don't need a legal team, a compliance consultant or an IT department to put this in place. You need one short meeting and an open conversation with your team about how they're already using AI.

Copy the policy above, fill in the blanks, and walk through it together at your next meeting. It's a step that's easy to skip, and it's the step that makes every other AI decision easier, because you'll have shared rules in place of unspoken assumptions.
