Can anyone tell you used AI? What AI detectors can and cannot do

By AI First Move · November 20, 2026 · All articles

AI detectors can't reliably tell whether text was written by a person or generated by AI. They make mistakes in both directions: flagging human writing as AI-produced and missing actual AI output. Before you trust a detector's verdict about your work, your staff, or something a supplier sent you, it helps to understand what these tools measure and why the score they give you doesn't mean what most people assume.

Key takeaways

  • No AI detector is accurate enough to use as the only evidence that something was written by AI. They flag human writing and they miss AI writing.
  • The risk of a false flag falls hardest on people writing in English as a second language, according to a 2023 study by Stanford University researchers.[1]
  • Even OpenAI couldn't make detection work: it withdrew its own AI text detector in July 2023 because of its low accuracy.[2]
  • Unedited AI output, often called AI slop, can make your business look careless. The issue isn't whether a detector catches it; the issue is whether it represents you well.
  • If you're accused of using AI and you didn't, your drafts and version history are your strongest defense. A detector score isn't.

How AI detectors work

What does an AI detector measure?

Many AI detectors lean on two signals. The first is how predictable the wording is: AI models tend to choose the most likely next word, so their text is very predictable. The second is how much the sentences vary: people naturally mix short sentences with longer ones, while AI output often has an even rhythm. Detectors combine signals like these into a score. That score isn't a verdict. It's a statistical guess based on patterns learned from earlier AI models, not necessarily the ones in use today.

Why did OpenAI retire its own detection tool?

OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT, released a tool for spotting AI-written text in early 2023 and withdrew it on July 20 of the same year. Its stated reason was the tool's "low rate of accuracy."[2] If the company that built one of the most widely used AI writing tools couldn't reliably detect AI writing, it's reasonable to be cautious about any detector's score.

Why do detectors flag human writing as AI?

Clear, simple writing is predictable writing, and predictable is what detectors look for. That's a real problem for business writing, which should be clear and direct. A 2023 study by Stanford University researchers showed how far this can go. Seven widely used detectors wrongly classified more than half of a set of essays written by non-native English speakers as AI-generated, while they were almost always right about essays written by US eighth-graders.[1] A detector can't tell "simple because it's AI" from "simple because the writer kept it plain."

What detectors can't do, and why that matters for your business

Can a customer tell if your content was AI-generated?

Most customers won't use a detector. But some will feel something is off without naming it. AI slop, meaning unedited AI output dropped straight into a website, email, or social post, tends to be generic, a little hollow, and oddly upbeat. Your customers know your business. They can tell when a "customer spotlight" reads as though it was written by someone who has never met a customer. That feeling can be enough to damage trust.

Does Google penalize AI content?

Not for being AI. Google's guidance, as of November 2026, is that it rewards high-quality content however it is produced.[3] What breaks its rules is using AI to turn out many pages that add nothing for readers, which Google calls "scaled content abuse."[4] So Google isn't hunting for AI. It's looking for content that answers what people are searching for. Use AI for the draft, add what only you know, and edit before you publish.

Should you accuse a staff member of using AI based on a detector result?

No. A detector score is an estimate, not proof, and acting on it alone is unfair to the person and risky for you. The better approach is a conversation about what you expect when your team uses AI at work, not an accusation based on a kind of tool that OpenAI itself couldn't get right. Set a clear policy on how your team can and can't use AI, and apply it from then on. The same goes for a supplier or freelancer.

Can a client or employer use a detector to reject your work?

They can try, but they'd be on shaky ground. Vanderbilt University, for one, switched off the AI detector in its plagiarism software in 2023, citing concerns about false accusations.[5] If your work is wrongly flagged, don't argue about how the tool works. Show how you wrote it. Version history in your word processor, early rough drafts, notes and research sources: those establish that the work is yours in a way a detector score never can.

Why detectors keep falling behind

Why can't detectors keep up with new AI models?

Detectors learn from the output of past AI models. Newer models write with different patterns, so every time a major model is updated, a detector trained on the older patterns can lose accuracy. On top of that, people who use AI sensibly add their own edits, examples, and voice to a draft, and that editing changes the patterns too. Work to improve detectors continues, but they are chasing a moving target. Detection on its own is unlikely to become a settled answer.

What to do about AI in your business writing

How should a small-business owner use AI for writing without creating problems?

Use AI for the draft. AI can produce a first version of your weekly newsletter, a product description, or a job posting much faster than starting from a blank page. But that draft doesn't know your customers, last week's price change, or the specific thing that makes your service different. Add that. Check the facts, too; AI gets things wrong, and there's a simple way to check its work. Then edit until it sounds like you. What goes out the door should reflect your business, not an average of everything the AI has read.

What is AI slop, and why should you avoid it?

AI slop is low-quality AI output published as it came. You recognize it not because a detector flags it, but because it's vague, a little breathless, and says nothing specific. A home page that describes a bakery as "a passionate team committed to delivering exceptional quality experiences" is AI slop. Anyone who has visited a real bakery will feel the gap. The risk isn't a penalty from Google or a complaint from a client; it's that your writing stops representing you.

How do you protect yourself if someone says you used AI and you didn't?

Keep a record of your process from the start, not after the fact. Save your drafts. Keep your research notes. Write in a tool that keeps version history. If you send work to a client or employer who cares about AI use, a brief note describing your process ("drafted by hand, revised twice, sources listed below") is worth more than arguing over a detector score. You answer a statistical tool by showing your work.

Customers, search engines, and staff

Will search engines get better at detecting AI content?

Possibly, but what Google says it acts on is quality, not who or what did the writing.[3] So trying to write something a detector can't catch is the wrong goal. Writing something useful is the right one, and it holds up whatever search engines or detectors do next.

Should you tell customers that you used AI?

That depends on the situation and on what you've promised. If a customer hired you specifically for your own writing, using AI without saying so is a trust issue. If you used AI to draft a routine update that you then checked and corrected, there is usually nothing to disclose. A practical test: would your customer feel misled if they found out? If yes, tell them.
