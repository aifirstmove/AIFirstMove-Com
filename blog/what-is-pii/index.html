What is PII? The personal information you should keep out of AI tools

By AI First Move · November 25, 2026 · All articles

PII, or personally identifiable information, is any detail that can point to a specific person: a name, email address, phone number, home address or account number. Health details about a person are a stricter kind, often called PHI (protected health information). Before you paste anything into an AI chat window, you need to know which details fall into these groups, because an AI tool processes whatever you type into it.

Key takeaways

  • A name alone is PII. So is a phone number alone. Details that seem harmless one at a time can become PII together.
  • PII hides in places you might not check: email signatures, spreadsheet columns, file names, meeting transcripts and screenshots.
  • Employee records carry PII just as customer records do.
  • If someone on your team has already pasted PII into an AI tool, there are steps you can take now.
  • Check how your tool and plan handle data before you use it for anything that involves real people.

What counts as PII in a small business

What is PII, in plain language?

PII is any piece of information that can identify a specific, real person. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology uses a broad definition: information that can distinguish or trace someone's identity, plus other information that is linked or can be linked to that person, such as medical, financial and employment details.[1] For a small business, that covers your customers' names, email addresses, phone numbers, home or delivery addresses, and any account or order numbers tied to them.

PHI (protected health information) is a stricter kind. If a customer tells you about a medical condition as part of a service request, treat that detail as highly sensitive. Whether it legally counts as PHI depends on who holds it. In the US, that label applies to identifiable health information kept by health plans, most health care providers and the companies working for them. For your own legal obligations, check with a qualified adviser. This page is general information, not legal advice.

Can harmless details become PII when combined?

Yes, and this catches a lot of people off guard. A job title isn't PII. Neither is an employer's name. But combine job title, employer, city and approximate age and you may well have described exactly one person. That combination is PII even though no single piece looks sensitive. The same goes for a customer's purchase history alongside their zip code and birth year. Don't judge each detail on its own.

What PII does a small business handle in a normal week?

More than you might think:

  • A customer's name and email in a support request
  • A delivery address in a shipping confirmation
  • A phone number in a voicemail transcript
  • Payment card details in an order record
  • A self-employed client's tax ID
  • A patient's appointment date and reason for the visit, which is health information

None of these belong in an AI tool you haven't checked.

Where PII hides in the things you paste into AI

Why do email threads and meeting transcripts carry so much PII?

Because people write and talk naturally in them. An email thread you paste in for a summary might contain the sender's full name in the header, their phone number in the signature, their company address in the footer and the name of someone else they mentioned. All of that goes to the AI tool along with the part you wanted help with. Meeting transcripts are just as dense. Speaker names, people mentioned in passing, figures and places all sit inside a file that looks like "just a transcript".

What PII hides in spreadsheets, file names and screenshots?

A spreadsheet with columns such as "Customer Name" and "Mobile" has identifying details in every row. The file name can carry PII too. "JaneSmith_Invoice_April.xlsx" gives away the person's name before the file is even opened. Screenshots are another source people overlook. A screen grab of your customer system or inbox might show names, email previews or account numbers in the background.

Does employee information count as PII?

Yes. Pay rates, performance review notes, disciplinary records, home addresses held for payroll, Social Security numbers and even shift schedules tied to a person's name are all PII. Don't paste those into an AI tool to draft a letter to a staff member or write a performance summary without removing the identifying details first.

A simple three-level sort for what to share with AI

How should I decide what's safe to paste into an AI tool?

Sort what you have into three groups before you paste anything:

  • Fine to use as it is: General questions, general business ideas, made-up scenarios and public information with no personal details attached. Example: "Write a refund policy for a small online shop."
  • Remove identifying details first: Real emails, real customer complaints and real meeting notes, but only after you've replaced every name, address and account number with a placeholder. "Reply to Jane Smith about order 4821" becomes "Reply to a customer about a late order." That swap is what it means to anonymize text.
  • Never paste: Health information, bank and card numbers, government ID numbers, passwords, and anything where exposure would do serious harm even after partial editing. If you're not sure whether something belongs here, treat it as if it does.

What is data retention, and why does it matter here?

Data retention is how long an AI company keeps what you type or upload. If you've pasted a customer's name and email into an AI chat window, that information may sit on the provider's servers for a period you don't control. Check how your tool and plan handle data before you use them for anything that involves real people. Our article on whether it's safe to put customer or business information into an AI tool covers what to look for, plan by plan.

What to do if PII was already pasted into an AI tool

Someone on my team pasted customer data into AI. What now?

Act promptly, but don't panic. Take these steps:

  • Write down what happened: Note the tool, the date and exactly what was shared (names, emails, order numbers).
  • Check the tool's data controls: Many AI tools let you delete a conversation in the account settings. Use that if it exists. Deleting a chat from your history doesn't always remove it from the provider's systems straight away, so read the tool's help pages on deletion.
  • Find out whether you have to tell anyone: Privacy laws in many places can require you to notify the people affected, and sometimes a regulator, when personal data is exposed. This is a legal question. Get advice for your location and industry, not from a general article.
  • Fix the process: One mistake usually means the routine needs a clearer rule. Write down what should and shouldn't go into AI tools and share it with everyone who uses them.

Can I use AI to help me remove PII from a document?

You can, with care. Using an AI tool to strip PII out of a document is circular: you share the sensitive details with the tool in order to remove them. If you've checked that your tool and plan keep your data private, that's a different situation from pasting into a free chat window. Know which one you're in before you try it. For a small business with no technical team, replacing names and identifiers by hand in a copy of the document is often the safer route.

Building the habit without a legal or IT team

What's the simplest rule to follow?

Before you paste anything into an AI tool, ask yourself: "If a stranger could read this window, would I be comfortable with what they'd see?" If the answer is no, anonymize the text or find another way to do the task. That one question catches many problems before they happen.

Should I have a written policy for my team?

Yes, and one page is enough. It doesn't need legal language. It needs three things: a plain list of what counts as PII in your business, a clear statement that those details don't go into AI tools without being anonymized first, and what someone should do if they think they've made a mistake. What laws should I know about, even in general terms?

A few privacy laws are worth knowing by name so you can ask your adviser better questions:

  • GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) is the European Union's privacy law. Selling to people in the EU can bring a business under it even when the business is based elsewhere.
  • HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act) is a US health privacy law. It covers health information handled by health plans, most health care providers and the companies working on their behalf.
  • CCPA (California Consumer Privacy Act) applies to businesses above certain sizes that handle information about California residents. Several other US states have privacy laws of their own.

Don't treat this list as legal guidance. It's a starting point for a conversation with a qualified adviser who knows your business and where you operate.
