Is it safe to put customer or business information into an AI tool?

By AI First Move · November 18, 2026 · All articles

Tools and settings checked November 18, 2026

The short answer: it depends largely on which plan you're using, and on what kind of information it is. Free and personal paid plans from most AI providers can use your conversations to train their models unless you turn that off. Business plans are different: they don't train on your data by default, and most come with formal data-handling agreements. The type of data matters just as much as the plan, though. Some information should stay out of an AI tool unless you have explicit legal clearance, regardless of what the terms of service say.

Key takeaways

  • Free and personal AI plans can use your chats for model training unless you opt out; business plans don't train on your data by default.
  • Personal details such as Social Security numbers, payment card data and medical records should not be pasted into any AI tool without first removing or replacing them.
  • A vendor's marketing page isn't enough. You need a Data Processing Addendum or equivalent contract terms to have enforceable data protection commitments.
  • Employees using unofficial AI apps or browser extensions create data risks that your official AI policy won't cover.
  • You can get most of the value from AI without sharing real customer data, by removing names and identifying details before you paste anything.

What actually happens to your data when you type into an AI chat

When you type into an AI tool, your input travels to the provider's servers, where it's processed and a response is generated. What happens after that depends on the plan you're using.

On free or personal paid plans, most providers reserve the right to use your conversations to improve their models. That doesn't mean a person immediately reads your chat. But it does mean your words can become training data, feeding future versions of the model. ChatGPT's free and personal plans work this way by default;[1] so does Gemini on a personal Google account, including the paid Google AI Pro plan.[2] You can usually turn this off in settings, but the switch is easy to miss.

Business plans work differently. ChatGPT Business, for example, is not used for model training by default.[1] No opt-out required.

Human review is another layer worth understanding. Some providers have human reviewers who look at a sample of conversations, for purposes such as safety and quality.[2] Business plans usually restrict this. Google, for example, says content in Workspace business accounts is not reviewed by people or used for training outside your organization without permission.[3] Check your own vendor's documentation and don't assume.

Personal plan versus business plan: the split that actually matters

The plan you're on is the single biggest factor in how safe it is to use an AI tool with business information. Here's how the major providers handle this as of November 18, 2026:

Provider Free and personal plans Business plans
OpenAI (ChatGPT) Free, Go, Plus and Pro: chats may be used for training unless you opt out in settings[1] ChatGPT Business and Enterprise: not used for training by default[1]
Google (Gemini) Personal Google accounts, free or Google AI Pro: chats can be used for training while the Keep Activity setting is on, which is the default.[2] On November 16, 2026 Google announced a separate switch for training, arriving over the following weeks[4] Google Workspace accounts: not used for training outside your organization without permission[3]
Anthropic (Claude) Free, Pro and Max: training is on by default, with a switch in the privacy settings to turn it off[5] Team and Enterprise: not used for training by default[5]
Microsoft (Copilot) Older Copilot app: conversations can be used for training unless you opt out.[6] The updated app released in August 2026: Microsoft says prompts and files are not used to train its foundation models[7] Work and school accounts, including Microsoft 365 Copilot: prompts and responses are not used to train foundation models[8]

Even on a business plan, you're still sending data to another company's servers. The question of training is separate from the questions of storage, access and security. Both matter. For what these plans cost, see How much does AI cost a small business? Free versus paid.

What you should never paste into any AI tool

Some kinds of data carry legal or regulatory obligations that go beyond any single vendor's privacy policy. These categories shouldn't go into an AI tool without explicit legal clearance, whichever plan you're on:

  • Personal identifiers: full names combined with Social Security numbers, dates of birth, addresses, or driver's license numbers
  • Health information: anything covered under HIPAA, including patient names, diagnoses, or treatment details
  • Payment card data: card numbers, security codes, expiration dates, or bank account information
  • Login credentials: passwords, access keys, or sign-in codes of any kind
  • Trade secrets or unreleased product plans: information whose value depends on it staying confidential
  • Children's data: anything that falls under the US children's privacy law (COPPA) if your service is used by children

These aren't arbitrary restrictions. HIPAA violations carry civil and criminal penalties. Failing the card industry's security rules (PCI DSS) can cost you the ability to accept card payments. Pasting this kind of data into an AI tool, even once and even by accident, can count as a reportable breach under some state and federal laws.

How to use AI safely with real business information

You don't have to choose between getting value from AI and protecting your data. Taking the identifying details out first is the practical middle ground, and it's simpler than it sounds.

Replace the specifics before you paste

Before putting any customer-related content into an AI tool, swap out identifying details with placeholders. Replace a customer's name with "Customer A." Replace a dollar figure tied to a specific account with a rough range. Replace a medical condition with the category of issue. The AI still gets enough context to help you draft a response or policy; your customer's data never leaves your system in recognizable form.

A simple find-and-replace pass on a document takes very little time.

Use fictional scenarios instead of real ones

If you want AI to help you write a complaint-handling script, you don't need a real complaint. Describe the type of situation: "a customer who received a damaged item and has emailed three times without resolution." Same result, no exposure. Keep a short internal template your team can fill in whenever they need AI help with a customer situation.

Keep a "safe to share" list for your team

Not all business information is sensitive. Your general pricing structure, your standard policies, your product descriptions and your tone-of-voice guidelines can all go into an AI tool with little risk. Write down what's safe and what isn't, and share it with anyone on your team who uses AI tools. A one-page internal document does the job.

Shadow AI: the risk that is easy to overlook

Shadow AI is what happens when employees use AI tools that your business never approved or even knows about. A team member installs a browser extension that "improves their writing." Another uses a free AI summarizer they found on a productivity blog. A third pastes customer emails into a free tool on their lunch break.

None of these appear on your list of software. None of them are covered by whatever AI policy you've put in place. And all of them might be sending your business data to servers you've never evaluated.

The practical fix isn't to ban everything. It's to give your team a tool they actually want to use, one that you've checked, and to make it clear that all AI use should go through that approved tool. If people reach for outside tools, it often means the approved option doesn't fit the way they work. That's worth understanding before writing a policy that nobody follows.

To spot shadow AI, ask. A regular check-in with your team about which tools they're actually using costs nothing and can surface problems early. If you have IT support, they can often see which new apps and browser extensions have been installed on work computers.

How to verify that a vendor's data promises are real

A provider's marketing page saying "we protect your data" isn't a contract. Here's what actually creates enforceable obligations.

The Data Processing Addendum

A Data Processing Addendum (DPA) is a legal document between your business and the AI provider that specifies how your data is handled, stored and protected. Under the European privacy law (GDPR), if any of your customers are in the European Union and the provider handles their personal data for you, you must have a binding contract of this kind in place.[9] Under various US state privacy laws, it's increasingly expected. If a provider won't offer one, that tells you something.

When you review a DPA, check for these four things:

  • Whether the provider acts as a "processor" (handles your data on your behalf) and not a "controller" (can use it for its own purposes)
  • Sub-processor lists: who else can the provider share your data with, and are they named?
  • Data deletion terms: what happens to your data when you cancel?
  • Breach notification timelines: how quickly are you told if something goes wrong?

SOC 2 reports and what they actually tell you

A SOC 2 Type II report is an independent audit of a company's security, availability and confidentiality controls over a period of time. It doesn't guarantee nothing will ever go wrong, but it does confirm an outside auditor reviewed the actual systems and processes, not just the marketing copy. Providers that have one will usually share the report under a confidentiality agreement. Asking for it is a reasonable step if you handle sensitive data.

Frequently asked questions

Does using incognito mode or private browsing protect my data when using an AI tool?

No. Private browsing stops your own browser from saving history, but it has no effect on what the AI provider's servers receive and store. That is different from the temporary or incognito chat mode built into ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini themselves: chats in those modes are not used for training.[10]

If I'm on a business plan and training is off, is my data completely private?

Not completely. Your data is still processed on the provider's servers to generate a response, and it may be stored for a time or reviewed for security purposes. "No training" means the provider won't use your chats to improve its model. It doesn't mean nothing is kept. Check the data retention terms in your agreement.

What if a team member accidentally pastes sensitive data into a personal AI account?

Act quickly. Most providers let you delete individual conversations, which removes them from your history, though removal from the provider's own systems isn't always immediate: at OpenAI it can take up to 30 days, and legal or security reasons can extend that.[1] Write down what happened, check whether that kind of data requires a formal breach notification under the laws that apply to you (a privacy attorney can advise), and use it as a reason to make your internal policy clearer.

Do I need a different AI policy for different roles in my business?

It helps. Someone handling customer support emails sees very different data from someone writing marketing copy. A simple two-level approach works: define what all staff can share with AI tools, and separately define what needs special handling or is off-limits entirely.

Are AI tools subject to HIPAA if I work in healthcare?

Yes. If the AI tool handles protected health information on your behalf, the provider must sign a Business Associate Agreement (BAA) with you before you use it that way.[11] Not every AI provider or plan offers one. Confirm this before using any AI tool with patient-related information.

Start with what's safe, then expand

You don't need to solve every data question before you get value from AI. Start with low-risk tasks: drafting internal messages, brainstorming marketing ideas, summarizing meeting notes that contain nothing sensitive. Build your team's comfort and your own confidence with inputs that have the names taken out. Then, once you're on the right plan and you have a DPA in place, you can move on to more involved work.
