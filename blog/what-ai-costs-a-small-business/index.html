How much does AI cost a small business? Free versus paid

By AI First Move · November 16, 2026 · All articles

The short answer

You can start with AI for $0. The main AI assistants each have a free version, the best-known paid personal plans cost around $20 a month, and most business plans run roughly $20 to $25 per user per month (see the price table below, checked November 16, 2026). Free plans work for experimenting. Paid plans make sense once you're running real work through the tool every week. Deciding when to move from one to the other is the part that takes some thought.

Key takeaways

  • Free AI plans are useful for getting started, but they come with usage limits, can be slower at busy times, and carry weaker privacy terms.
  • Paid plans typically charge per seat, per usage, or both; costs add up fast if you're not tracking what each tool is actually doing for you.
  • The subscription price is rarely the whole cost. Setup time, prompt writing, workflow fixes, and periodic updates all take hours you should factor in.
  • Switching AI vendors later takes time and money; picking tools with data export options from day one makes a later move easier.
  • A single AI chat tool and a spreadsheet are enough to start. You don't need a developer or a large stack.

What do free AI tools actually give you?

Are free AI tools good enough for a small business?

Free AI tools are good enough to start. Most aren't built to handle daily business workloads without hitting walls, though. You get access to the core features (text generation, summarization, basic question-answering), but free tiers typically cap how much you can use in a day or a month. Hit that cap mid-week and your workflow stops cold.

Free plans can also be slower at busy times, because paying customers get priority. And if client data or internal financials go into your prompts, free plans almost never offer the data privacy terms that business plans do.

Use free plans to figure out which tools fit your workflow. Don't build your operations around them.

What data privacy risks come with free AI tools?

On the personal versions of the main AI assistants, what you type can be used to train the provider's models unless you switch a setting off.[8] In other words, your text may become part of the training data. That's fine for drafting a marketing tagline. It's a real problem if you're pasting in customer lists, financial records, or anything sensitive.

One point is easy to miss: this applies to the individual paid plans too, not just the free ones. It is the business plans where the major providers say they do not train on your data by default.[8] Before you type anything confidential into an AI tool, check the provider's terms and the privacy settings on your plan.

What's the actual difference between a free and paid AI plan?

Paid plans give you higher usage limits, faster response times, and priority access during peak hours. You also get access to more capable model versions. Business plans add the data privacy protections described above, and some include team collaboration features and integrations with tools you already use, like your email platform or CRM.

The gap isn't just features. It's reliability. If a tool goes slow or locks you out every few days, it's not saving you time anymore.

How do per-seat versus usage-based pricing actually work?

Per-seat pricing charges a flat monthly fee for each person on your team who uses the tool. Three employees using an AI writing assistant means you pay three times the per-seat rate. Usage-based pricing charges based on how much output you generate, often measured in tokens (units of text) or API calls. Some tools combine both models.

For a small team doing moderate, predictable AI work, per-seat pricing is easier to budget. Usage-based pricing can be cheaper if you use the tool lightly, but it can spike unexpectedly if someone on your team runs a large project through it. Know which model you're on before the first bill arrives.

What do AI tools for specific business tasks cost?

General-purpose AI assistants are typically the cheapest. Specialized platforms built for a specific job (automating customer service responses, generating product descriptions from inventory data, transcribing and summarizing meetings) tend to cost more because they're doing heavier, more integrated work. Expect to pay a premium for tools that connect directly to your existing software rather than tools you use in isolation.

The more a tool plugs into your existing systems, the more it tends to cost, and the more it can save you if it's the right fit.

What hidden costs catch small businesses off guard?

What is the "setup cost" of an AI tool?

Setup cost is the time you spend before the tool starts saving you time. That includes learning how it works, writing the prompts it needs to do your specific job well, connecting it to other tools if needed, and training your team. None of that shows up on a subscription invoice. But if setup takes your team 10 hours, that's real labor cost you need to count when you decide whether the tool is paying off.

Some tools are quick: a general AI chat assistant can be useful the same day you sign up. Others, particularly those that integrate with your CRM or booking system, take considerably longer to configure. Ask vendors for a realistic setup time estimate before you commit.

What is "AI maintenance cost" and why does it matter?

AI models update. When they do, the prompts and workflows you built around the old version sometimes stop working the way you intended. That means rewriting instructions, retesting outputs, and fixing automations from time to time. It's a cost that is easy to overlook when you sign up.

It's not huge. But it's not zero, either. Set aside a few hours each quarter to review whether your AI tools are still producing the quality you set them up to produce. If you're using AI for customer-facing work, review more often.

What is the "rework cost" of using AI?

AI output needs human review before it goes out. Every time. At least at first. The rework cost is the time you spend reading, correcting, and sometimes completely rewriting what an AI tool produces. Early on, that cost is high: you'll catch errors, wrong tones, and inaccuracies that need fixing before anything reaches a customer.

Over time, as you refine your prompts and get a feel for what the tool does well, rework usually drops. Skipping the review step to save time is how wrong information or embarrassing copy ends up in front of a customer. Don't skip it.

What does vendor lock-in cost a small business?

Vendor lock-in happens when switching to a different AI tool means you lose your saved workflows, your customized prompts, your data, or your integrations. Rebuilding all of that takes time and money. Some AI platforms make it easy to export your data and move on; others make it harder.

Before you build real business processes around any AI tool, ask two questions: Can I export my data? Can I replicate this workflow on another platform if I need to? If the answers are "no" or "not easily," factor future switching costs into your decision now rather than later.

Free versus paid: how to decide

How do I know when to upgrade from a free AI plan to a paid one?

Upgrade when hitting your free plan's limits is interrupting real work, not just inconveniencing you. Three clear signals: you're hitting usage caps during actual business tasks; the tool's slowness is adding time instead of saving it; or you need to put sensitive business or customer data into the tool. Any one of those is enough. Don't wait for all three. For the third signal, choose a business plan rather than a personal one, because that is where the stronger data terms are.[8]

Can I build my own AI tool instead of buying one?

You can, using an AI provider's API (application programming interface) to build something tailored to your business. The upside is flexibility and potentially lower per-usage costs at scale. The downside is that it requires a developer, either on staff or hired, plus ongoing technical maintenance. For most small businesses without an in-house developer, off-the-shelf paid tools are the faster, cheaper starting point.

Should a retail business approach AI costs differently than a service business?

Yes. A service business (consulting, coaching, legal, accounting) often gets the fastest return from AI tools that handle writing, summarization, and client communication drafts. Those tasks eat large chunks of billable time. A retail business typically gets more value from AI tools that handle product descriptions, inventory-related content, or customer service responses at volume.

The economics differ because the time saved maps to different workflows. Pick the tool that fits the task you do most often, not the tool with the most features.

Is it worth paying for multiple AI tools, or should I stick to one?

Start with one. Get it working well for one task before adding a second tool. The temptation to build a large stack of AI tools early is real, and it multiplies both your subscription costs and your maintenance overhead. One AI assistant doing three tasks well beats three AI tools each doing one task inconsistently.

When you've reached the limit of what one tool can do for you, that's the right moment to add another. Not before.
