How to tell whether an AI tool actually works before you buy it

By AI First Move · November 24, 2026 · All articles

Run your own tests on real examples from your business before you spend anything. That's the short version. A vendor demo shows you the tool at its best. Ten real examples of your own will show you whether it does the job you need. That gap is where many buying mistakes happen.

Key takeaways

  • Vendor demos are staged. Your own tests, using your own material, are the most reliable signal.
  • Evals (short for evaluations) are the structured tests that separate real performance from polished marketing.
  • Hidden costs go beyond the subscription fee. Factor in setup time, staff training, and what happens to your data if you cancel.
  • A human in the loop isn't optional at first. Plan for it from day one, and you'll avoid many of the costly mistakes that come from trusting unreviewed AI output.

What you need before you start testing

You don't need a technical background to do this. You need a few things gathered ahead of time so your tests reflect your actual business, not an imagined one.

Here's what to pull together before you evaluate any tool:

  • Ten real examples of the task you want the AI to handle. Actual emails, actual customer questions, actual product descriptions, whatever the job is. Ten is a manageable number that lets you start spotting patterns.
  • Your own answers to those ten examples. Write out what a correct, good-quality response looks like for each one. You'll use these as your benchmark.
  • A list of the rules your business has to follow. If you handle patient information or take card payments, rules such as HIPAA and the card industry's security rules (PCI DSS) may apply to you. Know this before you hand any customer data to a tool, and take real names and details out of your test examples where you can. Our post Is it safe to put customer or business information into an AI tool? covers what should stay out.
  • A simple scoring sheet. A spreadsheet works fine. Rate each AI response: correct, partly correct, or wrong. That's all you need to start.

Step 1: Run your own evals before you trust anything you see

Evals, short for evaluations, are tests that measure how well an AI does a particular job. You run them the same way each time so results can be compared fairly. That's it. No technical expertise required.

The vendor's demo isn't an eval. It's a sales presentation. The person running it usually chose inputs the tool handles well.

Your eval uses your ten real examples. Give each one to the tool and record what it produces. Then compare each output with the correct answer you wrote in advance. Score it: correct, partly correct, or wrong.

Pay particular attention to hallucinations. A hallucination is when an AI states something false as though it were fact. For a business owner, that could mean an AI drafting a reply to a customer that includes a return policy your store doesn't have. Watch for them in every output you review.

After scoring all ten, count how many the tool got right without any editing. That number is your baseline. If you need to rewrite most of the outputs before they're usable, the tool probably isn't saving you time.

Step 2: Test the tool on your hard cases, not just the easy ones

Most tools look confident on a standard request. The real test is what happens when things get complicated.

Pull two or three of the trickiest examples from your batch. A customer complaint you've never quite known how to handle. A product with unusual specifications that's hard to describe. A question that depends on knowing a specific policy your business has. Give those to the tool and see what happens.

Does it make something up? Does it hedge? Does it ask a clarifying question? Each of those responses tells you something different about how you'd need to manage the tool in real life, and how much human checking you'd have to build into your routine.

Step 3: Check how the tool fits your existing software before committing

An AI tool that works on its own can still fail in practice. If it doesn't connect to the software you already use every day, every AI output needs a manual copy-and-paste step. Those steps add up.

Before your trial ends, ask the vendor one specific question: does this tool connect directly to your email, your scheduling software and your customer records? A direct connection means no workaround through a third product.

If a workaround is needed, test it. Don't take the vendor's word that it's smooth. Set up the connection yourself during the trial and run a few real tasks through the whole process.

Step 4: Work out what the tool really costs you

The subscription fee is often only part of your total cost. This step is easy to skip, and it's where much of the time goes.

Your real cost includes:

  • Setup time. How long does it take you or a staff member to set up the tool, write its instructions and connect your data? Those work hours are a real cost.
  • Training time. Who on your team will use this every day? They need to learn it. Build that into your estimate.
  • Review time. You still need a person checking the AI's work before it reaches a customer or gets acted on, especially at first. Count those minutes per task, then multiply by how many times a day you'd use the tool.
  • Troubleshooting time. Something will break or behave unexpectedly. Set aside a realistic allowance for it.

Add those hours to the subscription cost and you have what the tool really costs you. Some tools pay that back quickly. Others don't. For what the main AI assistants charge, see How much does AI cost a small business? Free versus paid.

Step 5: Ask what happens to your data when you cancel

This question is easy to overlook during a trial. It's also one of the most important ones.

Data retention is how long a company keeps what you type or upload. Some platforms keep your conversation history, your customer data and your uploaded files long after you stop using them. Others delete everything shortly after you cancel. You need to know which kind your tool is before you give it sensitive data.

Ask the vendor two things directly:

  • Can I export all of my data, including conversation history, before I cancel?
  • How long do you keep my data after my account closes?

If the vendor can't give you a straight answer, that's a red flag. You'd be coming to depend on a platform without knowing how you could leave it. Being locked in to a vendor doesn't just affect your data. It affects your routines, your staff's habits and possibly the rules you have to follow.

Step 6: Plan how you'll introduce the tool to your staff

A tool that works perfectly well can still fail in a real business because staff don't trust it or don't know how to use it correctly. That's not a technology problem. It's a people problem.

Three things that can reduce resistance:

  • Start with one person and one task. Pick the team member most open to new tools and give them one specific job to do with the AI. Let them build confidence before you roll it out further.
  • Show the team what the AI can't do, not just what it can. When staff understand that they're not being replaced and that the tool still needs their judgment to catch errors, they tend to take to it more readily.
  • Keep a short correction log. Every time someone fixes an AI output, they write down what went wrong in one sentence. After a few weeks, you'll likely see patterns. Those patterns tell you where to adjust your instructions to the tool or your own process.

Skipping this step can be expensive. A tool your team doesn't use consistently is a tool you're paying for and not getting value from. If your team has no written rules for AI yet, start with a one-page AI policy for a small team.

What to look for in the vendor's data security answers

General security claims aren't useful. "We take security seriously" is on every vendor's website. You need specifics.

Ask these questions and expect specific written answers:

  • Does your platform meet the rules that apply to my industry?
  • Is my data encrypted both when it's stored and when it's being sent?
  • Do your employees have access to my business's data, and under what circumstances?
  • Have you completed an outside security audit in the past year, and can I see a summary?

If your business handles patient records or payment card data, general assurances aren't enough. You need it documented. A vendor who can't provide that isn't ready for your business.

Red flags worth walking away from

Some signals are worth taking seriously however impressive the demo looked:

  • The vendor won't let you test with your own material during the trial period.
  • Pricing is based on how much you use with no upper limit, so your bill is unpredictable from month to month.
  • There's no way to export your data in a usable format before you cancel.
  • Customer support during your trial is slow or evasive. It's unlikely to get better after you pay.
  • The tool produced hallucinations on several of your test examples and offered no way to flag or correct them.

Two products built on the same AI model can also behave very differently. Our post on why the same AI model acts differently in different products has a checklist of what to ask a vendor about that.

Frequently asked questions

What are evals and why do I need to run them myself?

Evals are structured tests that measure how well an AI does a specific job. You give the tool the same inputs each time and compare its outputs with a benchmark you set. Running them yourself, with your own examples, is the most direct way to see how the tool performs on the work you'd use it for. A vendor demo doesn't tell you this. Your evals do.

How many examples do I need to test a tool fairly?

Ten real examples from your business is a practical starting point for spotting patterns. Include a mix of simple cases and your trickiest ones. If a tool fails on more than a couple of your hard cases, that tells you something important about how much manual correction you'd have to build into your process.

What does "human in the loop" mean in practice for a small business?

It means a real person reviews and approves the AI's work before it's sent to a customer or acted on. For a small business, that's usually you or a staff member. At first, every draft gets read before it goes out. Over time, as you learn where the tool is reliable, you can reduce that review for lower-stakes tasks.

What if I cancel and need my data back?

Ask the vendor before you sign up: can I export all my data, including history, in a format I can open without your software? If the answer is no or unclear, treat it as a significant risk. Being able to take your data with you protects you if the vendor changes its pricing, is bought by another company or shuts down.

Can I use a free trial to run a real evaluation?

Yes, and you should. A free trial is exactly the right moment to run your ten-example eval. Don't spend the trial exploring features. Spend it testing the specific task you'd pay the tool to do. Your trial period is your lowest-risk chance to find out whether the tool is worth anything to your business.

Start with your biggest headache, not the flashiest feature

Pick the weekly task you already dread, the one that eats a morning or piles up at the end of every week. That's your first test case. Build your ten examples around it. Score the tool against your own answers. And keep a person checking the work until you've run enough real cycles to know exactly where the tool is reliable and where it needs your judgment.

That's the whole method. No special software needed. A spreadsheet and the tool's trial are enough to start.
