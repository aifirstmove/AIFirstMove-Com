How to ask AI a question so you get a usable answer

By AI First Move · November 20, 2026 · All articles

Most people get a frustrating AI answer because their question was vague, not because the AI is broken. Give AI a bit of background, a specific task, and the format you want, and you'll get something you can use. No technical skills required. It takes a few minutes to learn.

Key takeaways

  • A prompt is simply what you type or say to an AI. Better prompts come from clearer writing, not secret words or tricks.
  • Four elements make almost any prompt work: background, a specific ask, an example or limit, and the format you want.
  • Reading your own prompt before sending it is a step that's easy to skip. If your ask is fuzzy on the page, the answer will be fuzzy too.
  • AI doesn't know your business unless you tell it. Context (the background you share) is one of the biggest levers you have.

Step 1: Understand what a prompt is

A prompt is what you type or say to an AI: a question, an instruction or a request. That's it. There's no special syntax, no coding, and no course required. The skill of writing good prompts is sometimes called prompt engineering, but that phrase makes it sound harder than it is. Good prompting is mostly good writing: be clear, be specific, and say what you want.

When you type "help me write an email," you're giving the AI almost nothing to work with. No audience, no tone, no purpose. The AI fills those gaps with guesses, and some of those guesses will be wrong. The more guesswork you remove, the more usable the answer becomes.

Short prompts aren't bad. Vague prompts are.

Step 2: Add context before your ask

Context is the background you give the AI about who you are, who your audience is, and what situation you're in. AI doesn't know your business unless you say so. This is a common reason a first draft feels generic: the AI wrote for an imaginary business, not yours.

Write two or three sentences you can paste at the top of any prompt. Something like this works well:

"I run a small bookkeeping firm serving independent contractors. My clients are mostly non-accountants who find invoicing stressful. I write in a friendly, plain-English tone."

Save that somewhere you can grab it fast, such as a note on your phone. Paste it before your ask every time, at least until it becomes a habit.

Yes, it feels repetitive. The main AI assistants now have a memory feature that can carry facts about you from one chat to the next, but what it holds depends on your plan and settings, so pasting your background is still the dependable way to make sure the AI has it. For more on that, see What does your AI remember about you?

Step 3: Write a specific ask, not a topic

This is where many prompts fall apart. People name a topic when they should be giving a task. There's a real difference between "write about late payments" and "write a firm but polite email asking a client who is 30 days overdue to pay invoice 42 by Friday."

Before you send anything, check your prompt for these four elements:

  • Background (context): Who are you? Who is your audience?
  • Specific ask: Exactly what do you want the AI to produce?
  • An example or limit: A sample, a word count, a tone, a deadline.
  • Format: An email, a bullet list, a job ad, a social post.

You don't need all four every time. But the more you include, the less cleanup you'll do afterward.

Step 4: See the difference with before-and-after prompts

General advice only goes so far. Here are three everyday small-business tasks, each with a weak prompt and a stronger one side by side.

Task 1: Chasing a late payment

Weak prompt Stronger prompt
"Write an email about a late invoice." "I run a small bookkeeping firm. Write a short, polite but direct email to a client whose payment is 30 days overdue. Remind them of the amount and ask them to pay within five business days. Keep it under 100 words. No threats, no apologies."

The weak prompt gives you something generic that you'd have to rewrite. The stronger prompt gives you something that should need only a light edit.

Task 2: Writing a job ad

Weak prompt Stronger prompt
"Write a job posting for a part-time receptionist." "I own a three-person dental practice. Write a job ad for a part-time front-desk receptionist, 20 hours a week. We want someone organized and warm with patients. List the main duties, the qualities we're looking for, and a short line about why it's a good place to work. Use bullet points for the duties."

Task 3: Writing a social media post

Weak prompt Stronger prompt
"Write an Instagram post about my new service." "I run a mobile dog-grooming service. Write an Instagram caption announcing that I've added cat grooming. Friendly and slightly playful tone. Under 80 words. End by asking people to book through the link in my bio."

Notice the pattern: every strong prompt says who you are, what you want produced, one or two limits, and the format. None of them needed magic words.

Step 5: Read your prompt before you send it

Read what you wrote as if you had never heard of your business. If anything is unclear, the AI will guess, and you may not like what it guesses.

Ask yourself three questions before you send:

  • Would a stranger know who this is for?
  • Is the task specific enough that there's only one reasonable way to read it?
  • Have I said what format I want the answer in?

If the answer to any of those is "not really," rewrite that part first. A little time editing your prompt can save you more time editing the answer.

Step 6: Follow up when the first answer misses

You don't have to start over if the first draft isn't right. That's one of the most useful things about AI chat: you can steer it in the same conversation.

Say exactly what needs to change. Not "make it better." Try these instead:

  • "Make the tone less formal."
  • "Shorten it to under 60 words."
  • "The third paragraph assumes the client is angry. Remove that assumption."
  • "Rewrite the subject line to be more direct."

Specific follow-ups get specific fixes. Vague follow-ups start the guessing again.

What to do with the answer once you have it

Read every draft before it goes out. AI makes plausible-sounding errors. It may get the tone wrong, miss a detail you didn't mention, or state something as fact that isn't. We cover how to catch those in Why AI gets things wrong, and a simple way to check its work.

Your job isn't to accept or reject the whole thing. Edit it the way you'd edit a draft from a new employee: fix what's wrong, keep what works, and send something you'd stand behind.

Over time you'll learn which tasks AI handles well on the first pass and which need a heavier edit.

Common problems and how to fix them

The answer is too long and rambling

Add a word or sentence limit to your prompt. "Keep it under 150 words" or "give me three bullet points, not paragraphs" both work. AI tends to write long when you don't set a limit.

The tone feels stiff or corporate

Name the tone you want. "Conversational," "warm but professional," and "direct and no-nonsense" all give the AI something to aim at. Vague instructions like "make it sound human" don't help much.

The answer is too generic for your industry

Your background isn't detailed enough. Add your industry, your typical customer, and one or two things that make your business different.

The AI asks a question instead of answering

That's usually a sign your prompt left something open. Answer the question; you'll get a better result. Next time, put that information in your prompt from the start.

The answer contradicts something you said earlier

Restate the rule clearly in a follow-up: "To be clear, the email must not mention the invoice amount." In a long conversation, AI can lose track of earlier instructions, so repeating the key ones helps. Why AI forgets what you told it explains why.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to use special AI wording in my prompts?

No. Plain English works. You don't need to open with "act as a" or any other formula. Clear writing about what you want is enough for everyday business tasks.

How long should a prompt be?

As long as it needs to be to include your background, your ask, and the format. A clear prompt of a few sentences usually beats a vague one-liner. Pages of instructions create their own problems, so aim for specific, not exhaustive.

What's the difference between context and the ask?

Context is background: who you are, who your audience is, the situation. The ask is the task: what you want produced. Think of context as "here's the situation" and the ask as "so please do this."

Can I reuse the same prompt for different tasks?

Yes, and you should. Keep a short list of prompts for the tasks you repeat every week: customer emails, social posts, job ads, meeting agendas. Swap in the details each time.

Should I say what format I want even if it seems obvious?

Yes. What seems obvious to you isn't obvious to the AI. If you want bullet points, say "bullet points." If you want an email with a subject line, say "include a subject line."

What to try this week

Pick one piece of writing you dread every week: a follow-up email, a social caption, a job posting. Write a prompt with the four elements: background, a specific ask, one limit, and the format. Read it before you send. Edit the answer and use it.

That's the whole move. No course required and no magic words, just clearer writing about what you need.
