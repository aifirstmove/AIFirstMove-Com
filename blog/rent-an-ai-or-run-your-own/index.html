Rent an AI or run your own? Open-source models explained for small businesses

By AI First Move · November 25, 2026 · All articles

For most small businesses, renting AI through a subscription is the right place to start. It's simpler, faster and needs no technical setup. But if your work involves sensitive client information or large amounts of repetitive text, running your own open-source model is worth understanding before you dismiss it.

Key takeaways

  • A hosted AI assistant is the sensible default for a small business with no technical person on staff. You're up and running in minutes.
  • Open-source doesn't automatically mean private. Privacy depends on where the model runs, not on what type of model it is.
  • Running your own model has no fee per question once it's set up, which matters if you process large volumes of internal text every day.
  • You can try a model on your own computer without being technical, using free desktop software built for that purpose.
  • You can mix the two: a rented service for everyday and harder tasks, and a model on your own computer for sensitive internal work.

What "open-source AI" means

An open-source model, also called an open-weights model, is an AI model whose inner workings are published so anyone can download it and run it on their own computers, instead of renting access from the maker. It's like the difference between using software that lives on someone else's server and downloading an app that runs entirely on your machine.

That matters for one reason: control. You decide where your information goes. But "open" covers a range. Some models are free to download and use however you like. Others restrict commercial use. Read the license before you build anything around one.

And "open" doesn't mean "private" by itself. If you download an open model and run it on your own computer, your information stays on that computer. If you use the same open model through another company's website, your information travels to their servers just as it would with any subscription service. The location is what creates privacy, not the label.

What "data retention" means, and why you should care

Data retention is how long an AI company keeps what you type or upload. A separate question is whether your chats are used to train future models. Both depend on the product, the plan and your settings.

This is where renting is often safer than people assume. The business plans of the main hosted assistants don't use your chats for training by default. Our post on whether it's safe to put customer or business information into an AI tool sets out the differences plan by plan. So "I care about privacy" doesn't by itself mean "I must run my own model". Running a model on your own machine is the most direct way to keep information under your control, but for many businesses the right plan of a hosted assistant is enough.

Who should run their own model?

Not everyone. Running an open model yourself starts to make sense when several of these are true for your business:

  • You regularly handle information that is legally or contractually sensitive, such as legal, medical, financial or staff records, and a hosted business plan doesn't meet your obligations.
  • You want to run AI over large batches of internal text every day, and usage limits or costs are getting in the way.
  • You or someone on your team is willing to spend an afternoon learning one new desktop tool, and to fix it when it breaks.
  • You work in a field where another company storing your business conversations creates a regulatory or liability risk.

If none of those apply, a subscription does the job. There's no prize for running your own equipment. And if you handle regulated information such as health records, ask whoever advises you on compliance before you choose either route. Keeping data on your own computer doesn't settle every obligation.

Can you try this without being technical?

Yes. Free desktop applications exist to let people who aren't developers run open models on their own computers. You download the app, it walks you through downloading a model, which is one very large file, and then you get a chat window that looks much like any AI assistant you've used. No typed commands, no coding and no server.

You type your prompt, the model responds, and nothing leaves your machine. Check the app's settings first, because some of these apps can also connect to online services. Even if you decide to stay with a subscription afterward, you'll have made the choice with first-hand knowledge.

Expect it to be slower than a hosted assistant, and expect a smaller model to make more mistakes. For simple work that is often fine. Drafting an internal policy, summarizing meeting notes and writing answers to common questions don't need the largest model.

Using both

You don't have to pick one option for good. Some businesses run both: a subscription for everyday and customer-facing work, and a model on their own computer for anything involving sensitive internal information.

In practice that means a browser tab for one and a desktop app for the other. The discipline is knowing which window to open before you paste anything in. For a team that handles both public marketing and confidential client work, that habit is worth building early.

Frequently asked questions

Does using an open-source model mean my data is private?

Not automatically. If you run the model on your own computer, your information stays there. If you use an open model through another company's website or API, your information travels to that company's servers and falls under its retention policy, the same as any other service.

Are open-source models as good as paid AI services?

For many everyday writing tasks, the larger open models come close. For difficult reasoning and careful analysis, the leading subscription services generally still have the edge. The model your own computer can run is usually a smaller one, so judge it on your own tasks, not on headlines about the biggest open models.

Do I need a technical person on staff to use an open model?

Not necessarily. If you can install software from a website, you can run a small open model. But if something breaks, you fix it yourself. There is no support team to call.

How do I find a provider's data retention policy?

Look in the provider's privacy policy or terms of service, and in the settings of your account. Business plans usually come with written commitments about how your information is handled. Free plans often don't.

Can I switch between options as my business grows?

Yes. Nothing stops you from starting with a subscription and adding a model on your own computer later, once you understand your needs. The instructions and routines you build carry over from one setup to another, though you may need to adjust them.

The plain verdict

Start with a hosted subscription. It's the right call for most small businesses because it removes the technical barriers between you and useful work. Once you've used AI on a few real tasks and you know which ones you do every day, you'll have a much clearer picture of whether the privacy or cost case for running your own model applies to you.

If it does, try a model on your own computer for an afternoon before you commit to anything. If the test goes nowhere, you haven't paid for software or changed how you work.

The goal isn't to run the most sophisticated AI setup. It's to solve a real business problem faster than you do now.
