Will AI replace my employees? What the evidence says

By AI First Move · November 25, 2026 · All articles

Short answer: Probably not in the way you're picturing. The research available as of November 2026 mostly shows AI changing which tasks a person does, not removing the person. The change is real, though, and it isn't spread evenly: routine office tasks and entry-level roles are feeling it first. Your job now is to work out which parts of your team's work are likely to change and which will become more valuable.

What researchers mean when they say a job is "exposed" to AI

When an economist says a job is "exposed" to AI, they don't mean the person gets fired. They mean some of the tasks inside that role can be handled or sped up by AI. A bookkeeper's job might be heavily exposed, but that doesn't mean bookkeepers disappear. It means much of what they do Monday through Friday can be sped up, handed to an AI tool or reorganized.

The International Labour Organization looked at how exposed each kind of occupation is to generative AI in a 2023 working paper. It found that only one broad group, clerical work, is highly exposed: about 24% of clerical tasks were rated highly exposed and a further 58% moderately exposed. For other occupations the exposure was partial, and the paper's conclusion was that the main effect is likely to be helping people do their jobs, not automating the jobs away.[1]

Keep that framing in mind. It changes how you plan.

What happens when real workers start using AI

Some of the clearest evidence doesn't come from predictions. It comes from studies of what happens when working people are given an AI tool.

A 2023 National Bureau of Economic Research working paper followed 5,179 customer support agents as an AI assistant was introduced. Agents with the assistant resolved 14% more customer issues per hour on average. The gain was largest for new and lower-skilled agents, at 34%, and small for the most experienced and skilled agents.[2]

A separate experiment by two MIT researchers, published in the journal Science in 2023, gave writing tasks to 453 college-educated professionals and let half of them use an AI chatbot. The time taken fell by 40% on average and the quality of the work, scored by independent evaluators, rose by 18%.[3]

Both studies measured people getting faster and better at their work. Neither measured people being replaced. The first one also carries a useful hint for a small business: AI helps a newer employee perform closer to the level of your experienced staff, and your experienced staff are the ones who can tell when the AI is wrong.

What the 2026 employment data shows

Two newer sources look at jobs directly.

The U.S. Census Bureau asked businesses about AI in a survey that ran from November 2025 to early February 2026. About 18% of firms were using AI in some part of the business. Among the firms that used it, about 96% reported no change in their number of employees because of AI, about 2% reported a decrease and about 2% reported an increase. When those firms described what AI did to their employees' tasks, 44% said it added to a task an employee still performs, and about 10% said it took over a task an employee used to do.[4]

The Stanford Digital Economy Lab studies payroll records from a large payroll company. Its August 2026 update, covering the period through June 2026, found no widespread, economy-wide job losses linked to AI. It did find one group under pressure: workers aged 22 to 25 in the occupations most exposed to AI. Their employment was about 19% below where it would have been if it had kept pace with workers of the same age in less exposed occupations. The gap came mainly from less hiring, not from more people being let go. No similar gap showed up for experienced workers, and the declines were concentrated in occupations where AI is used to do the task, not to assist the person doing it. The authors describe these as patterns in the data, not proof of cause.[5]

Put together, the picture for a small business is less "people are let go" and more "the junior role you would have hired for next year looks different".

One limit on all of this: most of these studies measure AI that drafts and suggests while a person does the job. AI agents, which carry out several steps of work on their own, are newer than much of the research. Treat the evidence as a picture of today, not a promise about the next five years.

Which parts of your team's work are likely to change

Not every role is affected equally. The table below sorts common small-business roles by the kinds of tasks the studies above found AI handles well. It's a starting point for your own thinking, not a verdict on any role or any employee.

Role or function Tasks AI can take on or speed up What stays with a person What it likely means
Administration and scheduling Calendar management, data entry, drafting routine correspondence Judgment calls, sensitive conversations, exceptions to the rule Fewer hours on routine admin; the role changes more than most
Customer service Answering common questions, routing inquiries, writing follow-up emails Complaints, empathy, anything unusual, local knowledge AI handles routine questions; people handle the harder ones
Bookkeeping and basic finance Sorting transactions, producing reports, matching records Tax decisions, cash-flow decisions, supplier relationships Less time on entry work; more time on advice and checking
Content and marketing First drafts, captions, search-friendly text, scheduling posts Your voice, the plan, knowing your customers The same people can produce more
Field, trade and hands-on work Route planning, job scheduling, ordering parts The physical work, fixing problems on site, customer trust The work itself changes little; the office tasks around it change
Sales Researching leads, drafting proposals, follow-up messages Building relationships, negotiating, reading the room More time selling, less time preparing

What the forecasts say, and what they can't tell you

In its Future of Jobs Report 2025, the World Economic Forum estimated that by 2030 about 92 million current jobs worldwide will be displaced and about 170 million new ones created, a net gain of 78 million. That estimate comes from a survey of employers and covers every large shift in the economy, including technology, not AI alone. The report paired it with a call for urgent retraining.[6] That isn't a "don't worry" message. It's a "prepare now" message.

What no forecast can tell you is what happens inside your business. That depends on the tasks your people do. Your cleaner, your plumber and your front-of-house manager do work that AI can't reach. Your part-time administrator who spends hours a day reformatting spreadsheets does work that can already be automated.

How to talk to your team before the worry builds

Your employees have read the same headlines you have, and the headlines aren't reassuring. If you bring in an AI tool without saying anything first, you leave your team to fill in the blanks, and they'll fill them in with the worst case.

Three things worth saying out loud, in a team meeting or one to one:

  • What you're bringing in and why: "We're going to use an AI tool for the first draft of our weekly client reports. The goal is to get that time back so you can focus on the parts of the job that need a person."
  • What you're not doing: "This isn't a plan to cut jobs. It's a plan to stop spending your time on things a machine can do." Say this only if it's true. If hours or roles may change, say that plainly instead. A promise you later break costs more than an uncomfortable conversation now.
  • That their input is part of the process: "I need you to tell me when it gets something wrong, because that's how we make it useful." This treats your team as the experts and the AI as the tool, which is accurate.

A tool your team distrusts tends to go unused. If you'd like the ground rules in writing, our one-page AI policy for a small team is a place to start.

A simple way to decide what to change

You don't need a consultant or special software to start. An afternoon with your team and a plain spreadsheet will get you a long way. For each role, sort every regular task into one of three columns:

  • Automate: Repetitive, rule-based and low-stakes. AI tools can handle this with light oversight. Scheduling, standard replies, reformatting data.
  • Assist: AI does a first pass, then a person reviews and approves. Proposals, customer emails, financial summaries. This is where most of your gains are likely to come from.
  • People only: Needs judgment, relationships, physical presence or accountability. Keep this in your team's hands.

Two things often happen in this exercise. There's more in the "Automate" column than you expected, and the "People only" column is longer than the alarming headlines made it seem. Most small-business roles are a mix of all three.

Do the sorting with each employee, not for them. People tend to accept change they helped design and resist change that was done to them.

New hires and veterans are affected differently

The two studies that share an author point the same way from different angles. In the customer support study, newer employees gained the most from AI help.[2] In the payroll data, the youngest workers in the most exposed occupations are the ones being hired less.[5] For your business, that has three practical meanings.

  • Your experienced people are still your most valuable asset. They know when the AI is wrong.
  • Training a new hire can get faster, because the AI can coach them through routine work.
  • The junior role itself needs a rethink. If AI does the simple tasks a beginner used to learn on, decide how your next beginner will build the judgment your veterans have.

Frequently asked questions

Will I need fewer employees if I adopt AI?

Possibly fewer hours on some tasks, not necessarily fewer people. In the Census Bureau survey, the large majority of firms using AI reported no change in headcount because of it.[4] Some businesses use AI to handle more work without adding staff. Whether your team needs to change depends on your workload, not only on what AI can do.

What if my employees refuse to use AI tools?

Resistance usually comes from fear or from a poor rollout, not from stubbornness. If a tool is confusing, slow to set up or makes people feel watched, they won't use it. Start with a small trial and willing team members. Visible wins do more than orders from the top.

Do I need to hire AI specialists?

For most small businesses, probably not. Many AI tools available in late 2026 are built for people with no technical background. What you need is someone on your team who is willing to experiment, make mistakes and report back. If you're not sure which task to try first, see where a small business should start with AI.

Should I be hiring differently because of AI?

A little. Roles built entirely on routine, rule-based tasks are the ones most likely to change. Roles built on client relationships, physical skills or solving messy problems are the most durable. That's worth weighing when you write a job description, without letting it stall a hire you need.

How do I know if an AI tool is making my team's work worse?

Read the output. Every time, at first. Quality problems in AI-assisted work are easy to miss if nobody is checking. Set a review habit before you set a target for using the tool. Our post on why AI gets things wrong has a simple way to check its work.

What all of this adds up to

Your employees are unlikely to be replaced by AI wholesale. Some tasks they do today will shrink or disappear. Some tasks they couldn't do efficiently before will become fast and easy. The parts of their jobs that need a person will change the least.

The businesses most likely to struggle aren't only the ones that ignore the technology. They're often the ones that bring in tools without telling the team what is happening, skip the step of working out which tasks to change, and expect results with nobody checking the work.

Start with one role, one task and one clear measure of whether it's working. That's enough. This article is general information, not employment law advice.
