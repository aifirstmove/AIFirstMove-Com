Teach your AI a repeat job once: what AI skills are and how to use them

By AI First Move · November 20, 2026 · All articles

An AI skill is a saved set of instructions that tells your AI how to carry out one particular repeat job the same way every time. You describe your process once, in plain language, and from then on you start the job with a single line. No retyping and no re-explaining. As of November 2026, ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Microsoft Copilot all have a feature called skills,[1][2][3][4] though whether you can use it depends on your plan.

Key takeaways

  • A skill holds the steps of one repeat job. A custom assistant, often called a project, holds background about your business for every chat. They do different things and work well together.
  • You don't write code to build a skill. You describe the job in plain language, let the AI draft the skill, test it on real work, and fix what's off.
  • The best first skills are jobs you already do every week: a status update, a customer follow-up email, a meeting summary, a quote or estimate.
  • Skills need looking after. When your process or your prices change, the skill has to change too.

What's the difference between a skill and a custom assistant?

A custom assistant, which ChatGPT and Claude call a Project, holds standing background about your business so you don't explain it in every chat. A skill is different. It holds the steps of one specific job. A skill doesn't need to know your whole business; it needs to know how to do that one task correctly, every time.

You can use both together. Your project knows you run a landscaping business and prefer a direct, friendly tone. Your weekly-update skill knows to list last week's finished jobs, flag any open problems, and keep everything to three short paragraphs.

One more thing worth knowing: most ChatGPT accounts lose their custom GPTs on December 11, 2026,[5] and Gems on personal Google accounts turned into skills on November 17, 2026.[6] Don't spend time building a custom GPT or a Gem now. For the background side, see our guide to building an AI assistant that already knows your business.

Which jobs are worth turning into skills?

Not every task belongs in a skill. Some jobs need fresh judgment every time, and forcing them into a fixed routine makes the result worse. Before you build one, ask three questions:

  • Does the job come around regularly: weekly updates, monthly reports, standard customer emails?
  • Does it follow roughly the same steps every time, even when the details change?
  • Can you describe a good result clearly enough that a new employee would recognize one?

Three yeses make a good candidate. Strong first skills for a small business include a weekly update for your team or a client, a follow-up email after a job or a sale, a quote or estimate from details you supply, and a meeting summary from your notes.

Leave it for now if the task needs a judgment call every time, involves a sensitive negotiation, or changes shape in ways that are hard to describe in advance.

Step 1: Describe the job before you open the AI

Do this on paper or in a plain document first. The point is to pin down exactly what you do now, because vague instructions produce vague results.

Write down four things:

  • When it happens. What starts this job? Every Friday afternoon? After every sales call? When a customer asks for a quote?
  • The steps. What do you do, in order? Be specific. "Write an email" isn't a step. "Open with a thank-you, summarize what was agreed, list the next three actions and who owns each, close with a friendly sign-off" is.
  • What good looks like. Tone, length, format, what to include, what to leave out.
  • An example. Find one real piece of your own work that you're proud of. If you don't have one, write a short sample now.

Give this step proper time. It's the easiest one to skip, and it's where the quality gets set. If describing things to an AI is new to you, How to ask AI a question so you get a usable answer covers the basics.

Step 2: Let the AI draft the skill

Now open your AI app and paste in what you wrote. Tell it something like this:

"I want to create a skill for [name the job]. Here is what I do now: [paste your description and example]. Please write a skill I can save and reuse, as a clear set of instructions to follow every time I start this job."

Read the draft carefully. Does it capture your steps? Does the tone match your example? Is anything missing?

Say plainly what to change: "The tone is too formal; my customers expect casual and warm." Or: "You left out the part where I always include a deadline." Keep going until it reads like a process you could hand to a capable new hire.

Keep a copy of the finished instructions in your own files. If an app changes how its skills work, you can rebuild quickly from your copy.

Step 3: Save the skill in your app

Each app handles this a little differently, and the menus change often, so look for a Skills page or setting in yours. In Gemini, for example, the Skills page lets you create a skill with Gemini's help, start from a template, or upload a skill file, and you can also create one from a chat.[3] Give the skill a name you'll recognize ("Weekly client update" beats "Skill 1").

Some apps let a skill carry reference files: your tone guide, a standard price sheet, a list of your services. Add them if you have them. If not, plain instructions are enough to start.

Check your plan first. Which plans include skills differs from app to app and changes over time. Microsoft's help page, for instance, says skills currently need its premium Copilot plan,[4] and Google's says skills aren't yet available in every country.[3] Your app's own help page will tell you what your plan includes.

Step 4: Try it on three real cases before you rely on it

Don't send anything to a customer yet. Run the skill on three real examples from your past work, where you already know what a good result looks like because you did the job yourself.

Compare what the skill produces with what you produced at the time:

  • Did it follow your steps in the right order?
  • Does the tone match your example?
  • Did it include everything it should, and leave out what it shouldn't?
  • Would you send it as it is, or is there a fix you'd make every time?

If you're making the same fix every time, put that fix into the skill's instructions. Three cases is a minimum. If the job varies a lot, try more before you trust it with live work.

Step 5: Use it, and read every result at first

Once the skill gets through your three cases, start using it for real jobs. And read every result before it goes out, at least at first.

A well-tried skill should get most things right. But AI can still misread an unusual request, miss something that wasn't in the instructions, or meet a case you didn't think of. You're the editor; the skill writes the first draft.

After a few weeks you'll know where the skill is reliable and where it still needs a human check. The goal isn't zero oversight. It's less time on the first draft.

Keep your skills up to date

Your tone changes. Your process changes. Your prices change. A skill that was right when you wrote it can quietly produce out-of-date work months later if nobody updates it.

Look over your skills every few months. For each one, ask whether it still matches how you do the job, whether its reference files have changed, and whether you're still happy with the results. When you change a skill, note the date and what changed at the top of your copy.

If you have staff, give each skill one owner. Everyone else uses it as it is and tells the owner when it needs fixing. Delete skills you no longer use, so nobody runs an old one by mistake.

When a skill isn't producing what you expected

What you see Likely cause Fix
The result ignores part of your instructions The instructions are too long or contain rules that conflict Shorten them and remove any rules that contradict each other
The tone is uneven or too formal The skill has no example, or the example doesn't sound like you Add a real example you're proud of and say it is the target
The result is fine but misses a recent change The skill hasn't been updated since your process changed Update the instructions and try three cases again
It works on simple requests but fails on complicated ones The instructions don't say what to do with unusual cases Add a short note on how to handle them, then try your hardest cases
A reference file isn't being used The instructions don't say when to look at it Name the file in the instructions: "Use the price sheet for all prices"

If none of these helps, cut the skill back to its simplest form and build it up again. Sometimes the clearest instructions are two short paragraphs, not ten detailed rules.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to know how to code to build a skill?

No. At its heart a skill is plain text: a description of a job in ordinary language. If you can explain a task to a new employee, you can write a skill. The AI helps with the rest.

Can I use skills on a free plan?

It depends on the app, and it changes. Check the skills help page for the app you use before you plan around it.

How is a skill different from saving a prompt?

A saved prompt is text you paste in to start a conversation. A skill is kept inside the app, can carry files and examples with it, and is there whenever that job comes up, so you start it with one line.

What if I want the skill to sound different for different clients?

Put a line in the skill such as "adjust how formal this is to the note I give you." Then, when you use it, add a few words: "Client is a law firm; keep it formal" or "Client is a local cafe; keep it warm and casual." The skill stays the same and your note adjusts the result.

Can I move a skill from one AI app to another?

Often, yes. Gemini and Copilot, for example, both accept a skill as an uploaded text file in the same format.[3][4] Even where a direct upload isn't possible, your saved instructions can be pasted into another app's skill.

Start with the job you dread most

Pick the weekly task you already resent, the one where you stare at a blank screen and rewrite the same email you've written many times before. Build a skill for that one first.

You don't need a full set on day one. One good skill, tried and corrected, saves time every week. Build the next one when you're ready.
